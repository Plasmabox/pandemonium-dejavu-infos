DÉJÀ VU

You've seen this before. A camera on my chest films the dancefloor; the screen below it plays the party back to you — not now, but seconds or minutes ago, at random, interrupted by subliminal flashes from films about time.

What the costume keeps, and what it doesn't
  • Video — never saved. Memories live a few minutes in the costume's RAM, are overwritten at random, and vanish for good when it's switched off. No video is ever written to the SD card.
  • Sound — never recorded. The microphone only measures loudness and detects the kick drum to animate the screen. No audio is stored, anywhere.
  • bitchat chats — shown and logged. Messages posted in the public #mesh channel and private messages sent to the costume are displayed on the screen and saved on its SD card (date, time, nicknames, text) for its owner. Only write what you'd be fine seeing on a screen and kept.
Nothing is uploaded: the costume has no Wi-Fi and no internet connection.

What you're seeing

Déjà vu, made literal. The costume is a machine that hands you a memory you didn't know you had.

LIVE

The present

For 10 seconds the screen shows what the camera sees right now — and quietly records it as a short clip into memory.

RECALL

The past

Then it digs up three old clips at random — maybe from 20 seconds ago, maybe from an hour ago — and loops each one for a random 1–15 s. You might catch yourself dancing earlier tonight.

GLITCH

The flash

Sometimes, at a cut, a quarter-second frame slips in: a still from a silent film, or a line like THERE IS NO SPOON or LIVE. DIE. REPEAT. A glitch in the Matrix.

The video is black & white and filmed in infrared: two IR projectors light the crowd invisibly, so the camera sees in the dark of the club. Around the video, a sci-fi HUD shows the machine's "mind": its memory bank, the beat of the music, the people it tracks, and messages sent to it over Bluetooth. Three 7-segment displays echo the Back to the Future time circuits.

The memory cycle

One endless loop: LIVE → RECALL × 3 → LIVE… A cycle lasts ~34 s on average.

Why a cycle, instead of a simple constant delay? The first prototype did exactly that — a continuous 15-second delay — but the chip can't encode and decode video at the same time (see challenges). The cycle turned that constraint into the aesthetic: déjà vu doesn't come smoothly, it comes in jolts.

The screen

7″ · 1024×600 · every widget is drawn live by the microcontroller.

TERMINATOR mode

Motion detection on whatever is displayed — live or memory. Moving blobs get red corner brackets with real measurements (velocity, size, tracking time). Optional red or cyan filter. No face recognition: nobody is identified.

Time circuits

Three 7-segment displays, Back to the Future style. Red DESTINATION TIME flashes the year of the film behind each glitch. Green PRESENT shows LIUE + the slot being recorded. Orange LAST TIME DEPARTED shows the slot and age of the memory being replayed.

The control panel

A long press anywhere on the screen opens it over the video. A long press, not a tap, so people touching the screen at the party can't change anything by accident. Tap outside to close it; it also closes after 4 s of inactivity. Every setting survives a reboot (stored in flash when the panel closes).

Glitch images

26 stills from films about time, on the SD card. At a cut, one of them may flash for a quarter of a second, in black & white like the video, while the red DESTINATION TIME display flashes the film's year. Hover to see the original colour.

Glitch quotes

Half of the glitches are words instead of images: 33 lines from films about time, stretched white on black across the video for a quarter of a second — with the year on the red display too. They work even without the SD card.

Software

ESP-IDF 5.5 in C, no Arduino. ~10 000 lines on the P4, ~1 500 on the C6.

Main building blocks: esp_video (V4L2-style camera capture), esp_h264, esp_lcd + LVGL for the HUD, esp_codec_dev for the microphones, NimBLE + libsodium on the C6. Custom code: the LIVE/RECALL state machine and memory bank, the HUD widgets, the beat detector, the motion tracker, a wireframe 3D renderer with anti-aliased lines, and a from-scratch bitchat peer.

Talk to the costume

The costume is a peer on bitchat, the Bluetooth mesh messenger that works with no internet and no account. Install it, stay close, and look for MORDREAD_COSTUME.

  • Messages in the public #mesh channel appear in cyan bubbles on the screen, with your nickname.
  • Private messages are end-to-end encrypted (Noise XX) — the costume decrypts them itself and shows them in amber. It can't chat back: it auto-replies (in English and French) with a link to this page.
  • Be nice: what you write is shown to the whole dancefloor, and both public and private messages are logged on the costume's SD card.

Build log

From the theme announcement to the party: ten weeks, in milestones that each had to work before the next.

2026-08-25

The theme drops

Pandémonium VII announces "Déjà Vu". Same day, the idea: a camera that films the crowd and a screen that plays it back later, cut with subliminal flashes from films about time.

late August

Which costume?

Brainstorm with Claude (desktop app): motion-capture suit, a body worn backwards, merch from a future edition, a Mandela-effect character, time travellers from films… Then the DIY idea wins: a futuristic suit with cameras that record the party and screens that replay it late.

early September

Shrinking the plan

First draft: a Raspberry Pi, two cameras (front and back), two big screens, a Bluetooth scanner. Pi prices had jumped, so a second-hand x86 mini-PC was hunted on Facebook Marketplace — then dropped: too heavy and power-hungry to dance all night. Old iPads considered too. The answer: an ESP32-P4 board that is both computer and 7″ screen, one camera, one screen, no soldering, no 3D printer.

One Amazon cart (board, IR camera, power bank, standoffs, velcro, cables, spare ribbon, 128 GB card). Same conversation fixes the rules: object detection out (too heavy), slow motion instead of strobe (photosensitive safety). Claude Desktop writes the Claude Code project brief, and keeps writing prompts for it until mid-September.

Toolchain & first light

Parts arrive; development starts in Claude Code. ESP-IDF pinned to the exact version of the board vendor. The chip turns out to be revision 1.3 while every vendor example targets 3.x — every build needs a different config profile. Factory firmware flashed, live camera on screen.

2026-09-11

The 15-second delay

First working déjà vu: grey RAW frames, hardware-JPEG-compressed into a ring buffer in PSRAM, replayed exactly 15 s late. Face detection with boxes re-aligned on the delayed frame. A memory audit, an infrared filter that froze the image, and a dozen dead ends (colour, YUV, hardware scaling) along the way.

2026-09-14 → 09-15

Reset & the H.264 pivot

Firmware restarted from a minimal base, split into components. A throwaway proof of concept showed H.264 would hold far more video in the same memory — but that encoder and decoder can't coexist. Result: the LIVE/RECALL cycle and the random memory bank.

2026-09-16 → 09-18

Challenges & solutions

Most of the work was here. Each one was diagnosed at the source before fixing.

Night vision can't be software-controlled

The camera's IR-cut filter and IR projectors switch on light sensors. The board's camera connector exposes the control pins — wired to nothing on this board (checked on the schematic).

Three opaque caps on the three photoresistors: permanently in night mode, zero code.

Raw video doesn't fit

15 s of uncompressed frames ≈ 230 MB, for 32 MB of PSRAM.

JPEG first (~50 KB/frame), then hardware H.264: 16 ten-second clips in ~10 MB.

Encoder and decoder fight for RAM

The hardware encoder's reference frame must live in the ~300 KB of internal RAM. With the software decoder open too, memory got corrupted and the decoder crashed.

Never open both: alternate LIVE (encode) and RECALL (decode). The constraint became the concept.

The decoder rewrites its input

Each replay of a clip had more errors than the last. CRCs proved the bundled decoder edits its input buffer in place — undocumented.

Decode from a scratch copy; the memory bank is never handed to the decoder.

Colour is a trap on this silicon

Sensor flips scramble the Bayer pattern, the ISP's YUV output was corrupt, and the encoder only accepts one input format on rev 1.3.

Embrace monochrome: raw sensor data, averaged 2×2, no ISP at all. Infrared is grey anyway.

A narrow field of view

The driver's only working RAW mode read a small centre crop (~70°).

Clone that mode's register set and change only the window: full sensor, 1280×960, binned to 640×480.

The pretty HUD cost 30 % of the frame rate

A semi-transparent full-screen frame over the video was re-blended by the GUI library on every frame.

Cut it into opaque borders + a per-pixel table applied during the grey conversion: 10.7 → 15.8 fps, same look.

A silent microphone

Peaks at −55 dBFS even when shouting. A bug in the codec driver silently reset the gain to 0 dB.

Re-apply the gain after opening the device (+29 dB). Beat detection then worked first try.

Upside down, mirrored, untouchable

The panel is mounted at 180°, the touch layer wasn't rotated with it, and the touch chip sometimes didn't answer at boot (the vendor code then rebooted the board).

Own display init: retry the touch chip, boot without it if needed, fix the mirroring.

Talking bitchat from a microcontroller

No library: the protocol was read from the app's source. iOS dropped the link every 30 s, then private messages needed a full Noise XX handshake.

GATT server added, Noise XX + ChaCha20-Poly1305 with libsodium, delivery receipts so phones stop resending.
